Learning Advanced Mathematics

The biggest misconception about advanced mathematics is that it's about doing harder arithmetic. It isn't. It's about learning new ways to think.

Here's a simple path.

1. Master the foundations

Before advanced math, be comfortable with:

  • ✅ Fractions and percentages
  • ✅ Algebra (solving for x)
  • ✅ Functions
  • ✅ Basic geometry
  • ✅ Trigonometry

Think of these as learning the alphabet before writing novels.

2. Learn the "why," not just the formulas

Instead of memorizing

Area of a circle = πr²

ask:

  • Why is that true?
  • Where does it come from?
  • Can I derive it?

Understanding lasts much longer than memorization.

3. Visualize everything

Most advanced math becomes easier when you can picture it.

Examples:

  • Functions → graphs
  • Vectors → arrows
  • Matrices → transformations
  • Calculus → slopes and areas
  • Complex numbers → points on a plane

Drawing pictures is often more valuable than reading equations.

4. Learn one concept at a time

Don't try to study calculus, linear algebra, and probability all at once.

For example:

  1. Functions
  2. Limits
  3. Derivatives
  4. Integrals
  5. Differential equations

Each topic builds on the previous one.

5. Solve many problems

Reading math is like watching someone lift weights.

To get stronger, you have to solve problems yourself.

A good ratio is:

  • 20% reading
  • 80% practice

6. Don't fear proofs

Proofs explain why something is true.

At first they seem difficult, but over time they become logical puzzles rather than intimidating arguments.

7. Learn the language

Advanced math has its own vocabulary.

For example:

  • ∀ means "for every"
  • ∃ means "there exists"
  • ⇒ means "implies"
  • ∈ means "belongs to"

Once you know the symbols, reading math becomes much easier.

8. Connect ideas to real life

Examples:

  • Linear algebra → AI and computer graphics
  • Calculus → physics and engineering
  • Probability → machine learning and statistics
  • Graph theory → social networks and maps
  • Number theory → cryptography

Knowing why you're learning something keeps motivation high.

Best free resources

  • Khan Academy – Excellent for building fundamentals.
  • 3Blue1Brown – Outstanding visual explanations of calculus, linear algebra, and more.
  • MIT OpenCourseWare – University-level lectures and notes.
  • Paul's Online Math Notes – Clear explanations with lots of practice problems.

A simple study routine (45–60 minutes)

  1. 10 min – Review yesterday's material.
  2. 15 min – Learn one new concept.
  3. 25 min – Solve practice problems.
  4. 10 min – Explain the concept in your own words, as if teaching someone else.

If you can explain it simply without looking at your notes, you've probably understood it.

The key to advanced mathematics isn't exceptional intelligence—it's steadily building one concept on top of another while practicing regularly. Even topics like calculus, linear algebra, and abstract algebra become approachable when broken into small, connected pieces.
